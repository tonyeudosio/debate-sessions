Why Do We Get Jet Lag?

Listening for detail + guided speaking · TED-Ed, ~4.5 minutes

Before You Watch

body clock — the internal system that tells your body when to sleep and wake
jet lag — tiredness and confusion after flying across time zones
time zone — a region of the world that shares the same clock time
to adjust — to change gradually to fit a new situation
drowsiness — the feeling of being sleepy
isolation — being completely alone, cut off from others
Read these six words once before watching. You don't need to memorise them — just recognise them when you hear them. This is the only "preparation"; everything else comes from listening.

Quick check

Second Watch — Hunting for Details

Now watch again (or in short chunks, pausing where you need to). This time the goal is specific facts. It's completely fine to replay a section two or three times — that's not cheating, that's exactly how this skill is built.

Detail · Cause and effect

Detail · Open answer

3. The video mentions a scientist named Michel Siffre and a strange experiment he did. In your own words: what did he do, and why?

Don't worry about perfect grammar here — focus on getting the fact right. We'll go over it together live.

Detail · Numbers

4. Try to catch ONE number from the video (a year, a percentage, a number of hours — anything). Write the number and what it refers to.

Speaking — Using What You Heard

These tasks reuse the vocabulary and facts from the video, so you're not inventing language from nothing — you're recycling what you just heard. Speak for 1–2 minutes on each; I'll listen and we'll talk, not "correct" in real time.

Task A · Retelling (easiest)

Retell the main idea of the video in your own words, as if explaining it to a friend who has never heard of jet lag. Use at least three words from the vocabulary list.

Tip: start with "Apparently, our bodies have an internal clock that..." — you don't need to remember every detail, just the shape of the explanation.

Task B · Personal connection

Have you ever travelled somewhere far away and felt strange or tired for a few days afterwards? Describe it briefly — where you went, how you felt, and how long it took to feel normal again.

This is the kind of question that often appears in real B2 speaking exams — describing a personal travel experience.

Task C · Your opinion

Some frequent travellers say the best way to beat jet lag is to immediately follow the new local time (sleep, meals, daylight) rather than fight it. Do you agree with this strategy? Why or why not?

There's no wrong answer here — this is about giving reasons and defending an opinion, a key B2 speaking skill.
