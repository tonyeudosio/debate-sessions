The Writing Exam

Two parts, one hour twenty minutes, forty marks total. This page covers what each task actually asks for, how it's marked, and gives you real practice with a model answer to check against.

First things first

How the paper is built

Part 1 is not optional — everyone writes the same kind of text. Part 2 gives you a choice, but only from a set of task types that rotate exam to exam.

Part 1

Essay — compulsory

Everyone answers this. You're given a statement, two notes on what to write about, and a blank third note for your own idea.

  • 140–190 words
  • Must give a clear opinion, with reasons
  • Must use both given notes + your own idea
  • Worth 20 of the 40 marks
Part 2

Choose 1 of 4 options

You'll see four task types on the paper and answer only one. Which four depends on the exam version.

ArticleEmail / LetterReportReviewStory*

*Story usually replaces Report in the "for Schools" version. Article and Email/Letter appear in both — so those two are worth the most exam-proofing.

How you'll actually grade it

The marking scale, in plain language

Every piece of writing gets four separate marks, 0–5 each, not one overall impression. A student can nail the content and still lose marks on language — the scale is built to show you exactly where.

CriterionWhat it's really asking
ContentDid they answer everything the task asked for — no missing points, nothing off-topic? Does the reader end up with all the information they needed?
Communicative AchievementDoes it actually read like the text type it's supposed to be? An email that sounds like an essay, or an article that opens "Dear Sir," loses marks here even if the grammar is perfect.
OrganisationIs it easy to follow? Are ideas linked together, not just listed? Paragraphing and linking words live in this box.
LanguageRange and accuracy of vocabulary and grammar. Small mistakes that don't block meaning are fine — the question is always "can the reader still understand?"

5 Strong

  • Every point covered, nothing spare
  • Reads naturally in its genre
  • Varied linking, rich vocabulary
  • Slips don't confuse the reader

3 Solid B2

  • Minor gaps or extra detail
  • Genre conventions mostly right
  • Coherent, some cohesive devices
  • Everyday vocabulary, controlled grammar

1 Still B1

  • Some misreading of the task
  • Genre conventions patchy
  • Basic linking only
  • Errors are noticeable but meaning survives

0 Off-task

  • Doesn't address the question
  • Reader is left uninformed
  • Below Band 1 in every other respect

Bands 2 and 4 sit between these — a mix of Band 1 and Band 3 features, or Band 3 and Band 5. A candidate averaging Band 3 or above is performing at genuine B2 level; Bands 1–2 still read as B1. You'll find a ready-to-fill version of this scale attached to every task in the practice zone below.

Part 1 · compulsory · 140–190 words

The Essay

An opinion essay built from a statement and two given notes, plus a third idea of the student's own. Everyone answers this one, so it's worth over-preparing.

In your English class you've been discussing student life. Your teacher has asked you to write an essay for homework.

Write your essay using all the notes and giving reasons for your point of view.

"Studying part of your degree abroad brings more benefits than drawbacks." Do you agree?

Notes — write about: 1. independence  2. career opportunities  3. ______ (your own idea)
  1. Introduction — paraphrase the statement, state your opinion clearly from the start
  2. Given note 1 — one idea, developed with a reason or example
  3. Given note 2 — same treatment, don't just list it
  4. Your own idea — a full paragraph, not a throwaway line
  5. Conclusion — restate your opinion in different words, close firmly

Swap it: essay register

I think...In my opinion... / I firmly believe...
But...However, ... / Nevertheless, ...
Also...Furthermore, ... / What is more, ...
A lot of peopleMany people / The majority of people
In the end...To conclude, ... / Taking everything into account, ...
Annotated sample — Band 5

Nowadays, more and more university students choose to spend a semester in another country, and in my opinion, the advantages of this experience clearly outweigh any difficulties.1

Firstly, living abroad forces students to manage their own daily life for the first time — from cooking to paying bills — which builds a level of independence2 that a normal semester at home simply cannot offer. Furthermore3, employers increasingly value candidates with international experience, since it shows adaptability and strong communication skills, so studying abroad can open doors to better career opportunities4 after graduation.

What is more, being surrounded by a different language every day is, in my view6, one of the fastest ways to genuinely improve it — far more effective than any classroom.5

Of course, homesickness and the cost of living abroad are real concerns. Nevertheless7, I firmly believe that the personal and professional growth gained abroad makes it a risk worth taking.8

1Organisation — paraphrases the statement and states an opinion immediately, so the whole essay has a clear direction from line one.
2Content — given note 1 (independence) is addressed head-on, not just mentioned in passing.
3Language — "Furthermore" instead of another "Also" — range in linking devices.
4Content — given note 2 (career opportunities) covered with a concrete reason, not just named.
5Content — this whole paragraph is the candidate's own idea, the required third note — missing this costs marks even if the other two are excellent.
6Language — "in my view" instead of a third "I think" — varying the opinion language on purpose.
7Organisation — "Of course... Nevertheless" acknowledges the other side before returning to the argument — examiners reward this at the top band.
8Communicative Achievement — the conclusion restates the opinion in new words and closes firmly, matching essay register throughout.

Language bank — opinion & linking

Opinion: In my view / As far as I'm concerned / I am convinced that Addition: Furthermore / Moreover / What is more Contrast: Nevertheless / On the other hand / Having said that Conclusion: To conclude / All things considered / Taking everything into account Emphasis: Undoubtedly / It goes without saying that

Watch out for

  • Copying the essay title as the opening sentence instead of paraphrasing it.
  • Skipping a given note because the student disagrees with it — they must discuss it, not necessarily agree.
  • Squeezing the "own idea" into one clause instead of giving it a full paragraph.
  • Contractions and casual words (a lot of, stuff, kids) — essay register is semi-formal.
  • Sitting on the fence — the task always asks for a clear opinion.
Part 2 · optional · 140–190 words

Email & Letter

One task type, two possible registers. The exam gives you an email or letter to react to, plus notes — your job is choosing the right tone as much as answering the points.

You have just received an email from your English-speaking friend, Alex.

"Hi! I heard your football team won the regional tournament — congratulations! Tell me how you celebrated. Also, would you like to come and visit me here in Manchester this summer? Let me know!"

Write your email, using all the notes.

Notes — write about: the celebration  ·  the visit  ·  ______ (your own idea)
  1. Greeting — Hi/Dear + name (informal) or Dear Mr/Ms Surname (formal)
  2. Opening line — react briefly to what they told you
  3. Respond to each point — in the order they were raised
  4. Weave in your own idea — naturally, not bolted on
  5. Closing + sign-off — must match the greeting's register

Swap it: informal → formal

Hi Alex,Dear Mr Brown, / Dear Sir or Madam,
Thanks a lot!I would be very grateful if...
I can't wait to...I very much look forward to...
Let me know!I look forward to hearing from you.
See you soon, / Love,Yours sincerely, / Yours faithfully,

Rule of thumb: know the person's name → Yours sincerely. Wrote "Dear Sir/Madam" → Yours faithfully.

Annotated sample — informal register, Band 5

Hi Alex,1

Thanks for your email — huge news about the tournament!2 To celebrate, the whole team went for pizza afterwards and our coach even let us keep the trophy in the changing room for a week, which was hilarious.3

As for visiting you in Manchester, I'd absolutely love to!4 I'm free basically all of July, so any weekend works for me. One thing though — do you think I could stay at your place, or would it be easier to book a cheap hostel nearby?5 I really don't want to be a burden!

Anyway, I can't wait to catch up properly and hear all about your own season.6 Let me know about dates soon!

Speak soon,
Sam7

1Communicative Achievement — first-name greeting matches an email between close friends.
2Content — reacts to Alex's news before anything else — the natural first move in a reply.
3Content — answers point 1 (the celebration) with a specific, personal detail, not a vague sentence.
4Content — point 2 (the visit) answered clearly and enthusiastically.
5Content — the candidate's own added idea (accommodation) — shows initiative beyond the two given notes.
6Language — contractions and casual phrasing ("can't," "catch up") kept consistent throughout — no slips into formal register.
7Organisation — informal sign-off matches the informal greeting used at the top.

Language bank

Informal open: It was great to hear from you / Thanks for your email Informal close: Write back soon! / Take care Formal open: I am writing in response to... / I am writing to inform you that... Formal close: I look forward to hearing from you Polite request: I would be grateful if you could...

Watch out for

  • Mixing registers mid-text — one contraction in an otherwise formal letter stands out badly.
  • Sign-off that doesn't match the greeting (Dear Sir/Madam... Love, Sam ❌).
  • Opening a formal letter like an essay ("Nowadays, many people...") instead of stating the purpose directly.
  • Missing one of the points raised in the original email — every point needs a response.
Part 2 · optional · 140–190 words

Article

Written for a magazine or website — the one genre where the exam actually wants personality. Grab attention, stay engaging, round it off.

You see this notice on an English-language website for teenagers:

"Free time — what do you and your friends do to relax after a busy week at school? Write us an article about your favourite way to unwind, and tell us why you'd recommend it. The best articles will be published on the site."

Write your article.

  1. Title — short, specific, makes someone want to click
  2. Hook — a question, direct address, or surprising line
  3. Body 1 — the main idea, with a real example
  4. Body 2 — a second angle or a practical tip
  5. Round off — echo the opening, invite the reader in

Swap it: flat → engaging

Free time is important.Ever get to Friday and feel completely drained?
I like reading.There's nothing quite like losing yourself in a good book.
This is good for teenagers.If you never seem to switch off, this one's for you.
In conclusion,So, next time the week wears you out, why not give it a try?
Annotated sample — Band 5

Why Baking Is My Favourite Way to Switch Off

Ever get to Friday evening and feel like your brain has simply stopped working?1 For me, the answer isn't scrolling through my phone — it's flour, butter and a mixing bowl.2

Baking forces you to slow down. You can't rush a loaf of bread or a batch of cookies, and that's exactly the point: for an hour or two, homework and exam stress genuinely disappear.3 There's also something deeply satisfying about ending up with a result you can share — nothing beats watching your family devour a cake you made from scratch!4

If you've never tried it, start small.5 A simple batch of chocolate chip cookies takes under thirty minutes and barely any skill, yet it still feels like a real achievement.

So, next time the week has completely worn you out, put your phone down and preheat the oven instead.6 Trust me — your Friday self will thank you.7

1Communicative Achievement — a rhetorical question hooks the reader in the very first line, a classic article opener.
2Organisation — "isn't X — it's Y" gives a punchy, magazine-style rhythm to the opening.
3Content — answers the notice's actual question directly, with a specific reason.
4Language — vivid, informal vocabulary ("devour," "from scratch") suited to article register.
5Communicative Achievement — direct address to the reader ("you") plus a practical tip — engages rather than just informs.
6Organisation — the conclusion echoes the opening image (phone / oven) instead of just summarising — a strong closing technique.
7Communicative Achievement — a light, personal closing line keeps the friendly magazine voice right to the end.

Language bank

Hooks: Have you ever...? / Picture this: / Believe it or not,... Vivid adjectives: breathtaking / surprisingly simple / downright addictive Direct address: If you're anything like me,... / You might be thinking... Rounding off: So, why not give it a try? / Whatever you choose,...

Watch out for

  • No title, or a bland one ("My Free Time Essay").
  • Opening with "In this article I will talk about..." — too academic for the genre.
  • Never actually addressing the reader directly ("you").
  • Drifting away from the notice's real question.
  • Ending abruptly instead of rounding the idea off.
Part 2 · optional (standard version) · 140–190 words

Report

Written for someone with authority to act on it — a principal, a manager, a committee. Objective, headed, and built to be scanned, not enjoyed.

You have been asked by your college principal to write a report about the sports facilities at your college.

You should say what facilities are currently available, describe any problems, and suggest improvements.

Write your report.

  1. Heading — "Report on..." states the subject plainly
  2. Introduction — purpose and method in a sentence or two
  3. Findings, subheaded — group by theme, not chronology
  4. Second findings section — problems or gaps identified
  5. Recommendations — clear, actionable, easy to scan

Swap it: opinion → neutral report register

I think the gym is rubbish.The gym appears to be in need of significant improvement.
Everyone says...The majority of students surveyed reported...
You should build a new hall.It is recommended that a new hall be built.
Basically,...Overall,...
Annotated sample — Band 5

Report on After-School Clubs

Introduction
The purpose of this report is to assess the current range of after-school clubs available to students and to suggest possible improvements, based on a short survey completed by 40 pupils.1

Current Provision
At present, the school offers three clubs: football, chess and drama.2 According to the survey, football is by far the most popular. However, several students noted that no clubs are currently available for anyone interested in art or coding, which limits choice considerably.3

Areas for Improvement
The main issue raised was timing: most clubs run immediately after lessons, which clashes with the school bus for pupils who live further away.4 A number of students also mentioned that the drama club lacks proper rehearsal space.

Recommendations
Based on these findings, it is recommended that the school introduce a coding club, adjust club timings to avoid the bus clash, and allocate a larger room for drama rehearsals.5

1Organisation — headings plus a clear statement of purpose and method set report register from the first line.
2Content — factual, itemised information delivered plainly, with no personal opinion language.
3Content — the gap identified is backed by survey evidence, not a personal complaint.
4Communicative Achievement — impersonal phrasing ("was raised," "was noted") is exactly the register a report needs — an article or email would sound wrong here.
5Organisation — a clearly headed Recommendations section with distinct, actionable points — easy for a busy reader to scan.

Language bank

Purpose: The purpose of this report is to... / This report aims to... Reporting findings: It was found that... / Respondents indicated that... Recommending: It is recommended that... / The school could consider... Headings: Introduction / Findings / Areas for Improvement / Recommendations

Watch out for

  • Writing in first-person opinion throughout ("I think... I feel...").
  • No headings — a report should never look like a wall of text.
  • Asserting things without framing them as findings ("The gym is bad" vs "The gym was reported to be...").
  • Missing a clearly separate Recommendations section.
  • A chatty, informal tone that belongs in an email, not a report.
Part 2 · optional · 140–190 words

Review

Part description, part verdict. The reader wants to know what something was like and whether they should bother — both halves need to be there.

You see this announcement on an English-language website:

"Reviews wanted! Have you visited a café, restaurant or shop recently that you'd recommend — or wouldn't? Write us a review saying what it was like, and whether other readers should go."

Write your review.

  1. Title — tells the reader what's being reviewed
  2. Intro — what it is, brief context
  3. Description — what you actually experienced
  4. Evaluation — strengths and weaknesses, balanced
  5. Verdict — clear recommendation, who it suits

Swap it: bland → evaluative

The food was good.The food was absolutely mouth-watering.
It was expensive.It was a little overpriced for what you get.
I recommend it.I'd wholeheartedly recommend it to anyone who...
It was bad.It fell rather flat, unfortunately.
Annotated sample — Band 5

The Rusty Kettle: Worth the Queue?

Tucked away on a side street near my school, The Rusty Kettle has quickly become the place everyone's talking about — and after finally trying it last weekend, I can see why.1

The moment you walk in, the smell of freshly baked cinnamon rolls hits you, and the cosy, mismatched furniture makes it feel more like a friend's living room than a café.2 I ordered the hot chocolate with marshmallows, which was rich without being sickly, and a slice of carrot cake that might be the best I've ever had.3

That said, it isn't perfect: service was painfully slow on a busy Saturday afternoon, and prices are a little steeper than most cafés nearby.4

Still, if you're after somewhere cosy to study or catch up with friends, and you don't mind waiting a little, I'd wholeheartedly recommend giving it a try.5 Just maybe avoid weekend afternoons!

1Communicative Achievement — title plus a hook line establishes context before the evaluation even starts.
2Language — sensory, descriptive vocabulary creates atmosphere — a hallmark of strong reviews.
3Content — specific items named and evaluated individually, not a vague "the food was nice."
4Organisation — "That said" signals balance — strengths and weaknesses both covered, which a fair review needs.
5Communicative Achievement — a clear final verdict aimed at a specific kind of reader — the genre's defining move.

Language bank

Positive: mouth-watering / breathtaking / a hidden gem Negative: a letdown / overpriced / underwhelming Comparing: nowhere near as good as / far superior to Recommending: well worth a visit / I'd give it a miss

Watch out for

  • Pure description with no personal opinion at all.
  • Only positives, or only negatives — a fair review balances both.
  • No clear recommendation at the end.
  • Missing a title.
  • Sounding like a report — lists of facts instead of an evaluative voice.
Part 2 · optional (Schools version) · 140–190 words

Story

A given opening line plus two required elements to weave in naturally. Tense control does more work here than in any other task type.

You have seen this announcement in an English-language magazine for teenagers.

Your story must begin with this sentence: "As soon as Maya stepped off the train, she knew something wasn't right."

Your story must include:

a decision  ·  a stranger
  1. Opening — use the given line to set the scene immediately
  2. Development — build tension, work the required elements in naturally
  3. Turning point — a complication or decision
  4. Climax — the peak moment
  5. Resolution — a real ending, not a fade-out

Swap it: flat narration → vivid narrative

She was scared.Her heart hammered against her ribs.
Then something happened.Without warning, the door creaked open.
She waited two hours before he came.She had been waiting for nearly two hours when he finally arrived.
The end.[close on a final image or realisation instead]
Annotated sample — Band 5

As soon as Maya stepped off the train, she knew something wasn't right.1

The platform was completely empty, and the station clock had stopped at half past three — the exact time she'd texted her sister to say she'd arrived.2 She pulled out her phone. No signal.

Trying to stay calm, she followed the exit signs down a narrow corridor, her footsteps echoing far too loudly. That was when she noticed the stranger sitting alone on a bench, watching her.3 He had been waiting there for a while, judging by the coffee cup, now completely cold, resting by his feet.4

"You're not supposed to be here yet," he said quietly. "The next train doesn't arrive for another hour."

Maya froze. She had to decide, right there, whether to trust a total stranger or walk back into the dark, empty street alone.5 Taking a slow breath, she sat down beside him.

"Then you'd better tell me what's going on," she said.6

1Content — opens with the exact given first line, as the task requires.
2Language — past perfect ("had stopped," "she'd texted") correctly sequences events relative to the main timeline.
3Content — the required element (a stranger) enters naturally within the action, not bolted on.
4Language — past perfect continuous used correctly for background action — a mark of real narrative control.
5Content — the required element (a decision) is explicit and central to the tension, not incidental.
6Communicative Achievement — ending on a line of dialogue leaves the story open and intriguing, engaging the reader right to the last word.

Language bank

Tenses: past simple (main events) / past continuous (background) / past perfect (earlier events) Descriptive verbs: crept / hammered / glanced / froze Building tension: Without warning,... / Suddenly,... / Little did she know... Pacing: short sentences for tension, longer ones for description

Watch out for

  • Forgetting one of the required elements from the prompt.
  • Past simple only, throughout — flat, with no real sense of sequence.
  • Not actually starting with the given first line.
  • A rushed or unclear ending.
  • Dialogue without correct punctuation (new line per speaker, quotation marks).

Essay — the environment

140–190 words

In your English class you've been talking about the environment. Your teacher has asked you to write an essay: "Schools should do more to teach students about protecting the environment." Do you agree? Notes: 1. recycling  2. saving energy  3. ______ (your own idea)

Aim for 140–190 words0 words
Show model answer & marking notes

Protecting the environment concerns everyone, and in my opinion, schools have a real responsibility to teach students how to do their part.

To begin with, most students only learn to recycle properly if someone shows them how — separating paper, plastic and food waste isn't always obvious, and schools are perfectly placed to build that habit early. Equally, lessons on saving energy, such as simply switching off lights or unplugging devices, could make a genuine difference if repeated daily across an entire school.

What is more, I believe schools should also teach students to question the products they buy, since reducing consumption often matters more than recycling what's already been bought.

To conclude, while parents also play a role, schools reach every student equally, which makes them the ideal place to build these habits for life.

Quick check: clear opinion from line one · both given notes developed, not listed · own idea gets its own paragraph · varied linking (To begin with / Equally / What is more / To conclude).

Article — technology that changed your life

140–190 words

You see this notice on a website: "Technology — how has a piece of technology changed the way you live? Write us an article!" Write your article.

Aim for 140–190 words0 words
Show model answer & marking notes

The Tiny Device That Changed My Mornings

Would you believe a five-euro alarm clock changed my entire routine? It sounds unlikely, but let me explain.

For years, I used my phone as an alarm — which meant scrolling through messages before I'd even got out of bed. When I switched to an old-fashioned clock instead, something surprising happened: my mornings got calmer, and I found myself reaching for my phone an hour later than before.

It wasn't really about the technology itself, but about what it replaced. Sometimes the most life-changing gadget is the simplest one, precisely because of what it lets you avoid.

So if your mornings feel chaotic, maybe the answer isn't a newer piece of tech — it might be an older one.

Quick check: title makes a promise · opens with a hook, not a fact statement · personal, specific example · direct address to reader in the closing line.

Report — the school canteen

140–190 words

Your English teacher has asked you to write a report about the canteen at your school, describing the current situation and suggesting improvements.

Aim for 140–190 words0 words
Show model answer & marking notes

Report on the School Canteen

Introduction
This report evaluates the current canteen service and outlines suggestions for improvement, based on informal feedback gathered from classmates.

Current Situation
The canteen currently offers a fixed daily menu with little variety, and vegetarian options are limited to a single dish. Queuing times during the lunch break are also considerably longer than most students would like.

Recommendations
It is recommended that the canteen introduce a rotating weekly menu, add at least one further vegetarian option daily, and open a second serving point to reduce queuing times.

Overall, these changes would likely improve student satisfaction considerably at a relatively low cost.

Quick check: headings present · no first-person opinion language · findings framed neutrally · Recommendations section is separate and actionable.

An App That Actually Gets Me Studying

I'll admit it: I downloaded this flashcard app expecting to delete it within a week, like most study apps. Six months later, I still use it daily.

What sets it apart is how simple it is — you type in whatever you're learning, and it quietly reminds you to review it right before you'd normally forget it. No flashy animations, no distracting notifications, just genuinely useful timing.

Your story must begin with this sentence: "Tom checked his phone one last time before switching it off completely." Your story must include: a secret and a phone call.

Tom checked his phone one last time before switching it off completely. He'd been carrying the secret for three days now, and he was fairly sure it was written all over his face.

His sister had trusted him with the truth about the surprise party, and every time his mum asked a casual question, he panicked slightly more than the last time. He'd nearly given it away twice already.

Just as he'd finally relaxed, his phone buzzed back to life against his will — a battery he hadn't managed to fully drain. It was his mum calling. He hesitated, then answered.

"Tom," she said slowly, "you're a terrible liar, you know that?" There was a pause, then she laughed. "I already know. Your sister told me an hour ago."
