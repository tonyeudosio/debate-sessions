The Return of the Night Train

For a couple of decades, it looked as though the sleeper train was destined to disappear from Europe altogether. Budget airlines had made flying absurdly cheap, and one by one, historic overnight routes between capital cities were quietly cancelled, their carriages sold off or left to rust in sidings. Anyone who wanted to travel from Paris to Vienna, or from Zurich to Stockholm, without boarding a plane, faced a tangle of connections and an uncomfortably early alarm clock.

And yet, in the last few years, something rather unexpected has happened: the night train is making a comeback. New operators have launched routes that had not existed in a generation, and several state railways have reinvested in rolling stock they had all but written off. What changed was not, as some assume, a single dramatic event, but rather a slow accumulation of pressures. Younger travellers, in particular, have grown uneasy about the environmental cost of short-haul flights, and many now regard a night on a train — arriving rested, in the centre of a city, having slept through the boring part of the journey — as the more civilised option, not merely the more responsible one.

None of this means the revival has been straightforward. Building new carriages takes years, and the companies behind these projects have had to contend with ageing tracks, border bureaucracy, and, in some cases, a public that had simply forgotten these services existed. One route planner for a Austrian operator admitted that the hardest part of her job was not engineering but persuasion: convincing investors, and indeed her own board, that people would actually pay a premium to travel more slowly.

She needn't have worried. Tickets on several of the new routes now sell out months in advance, particularly for the private compartments, which come with a fold-down washbasin and, on the newest trains, a discreet reading lamp for each berth. Prices are not cheap — often comparable to a short flight plus a hotel night — but passengers seem willing to pay for the experience as much as the transport. Social media has played its part here too: a certain kind of dreamy photograph, taken from a train window at dawn somewhere in the Alps, has become its own quiet form of advertising.

Sceptics point out that night trains will never fully replace short-haul flights, and they are almost certainly right. The economics simply don't work for every route, and some travellers will always prioritise speed above all else. But the sleeper's revival suggests something more modest and, in a way, more interesting: that a mode of transport once written off as a relic can be reinvented not by pretending the last twenty years didn't happen, but by quietly acknowledging what people had come to miss about it.

Part 6Cross-text Multiple Matching

You are going to read four short extracts in which people give their opinions on working from home. For questions 7–10, choose from the writers A–D.

Focus: comparing opinions across texts — agreement, contrast and shared concerns.
A — Marta

I resisted working from home for years, convinced I'd get nothing done without colleagues around me. I was wrong. My concentration has actually improved, and I no longer waste an hour a day commuting. What I do miss, though, is the sense of belonging to something — those small, unplanned conversations that somehow used to solve half my problems before lunch.

B — Denis

My company went fully remote three years ago, and I still haven't adjusted. It isn't the isolation that bothers me — I'm naturally quite happy with my own company — but the blurring of boundaries. My laptop is always within reach, and I've noticed I now reply to messages at ten at night without a second thought, something I'd never have done in an office.

C — Priya

People assume remote workers are less productive, but in my experience the opposite is true, at least for focused, individual tasks. Where I do notice a real cost is in mentoring younger colleagues. It's far harder to teach someone the unwritten rules of a job over a video call than it is sitting beside them, and I worry that juniors are quietly missing out.

D — Tomasz

I'll admit I was sceptical at first, expecting my output to suffer without the structure of an office day. Instead, I've found I get through my work faster than ever. The trade-off, as far as I can tell, is entirely social: I simply don't run into people by accident any more, and those chance encounters, it turns out, mattered more than I realised.

Waiting for the Light

Elena Marsh never set out to become a wildlife photographer. She studied marine biology, and it was only while doing fieldwork on a remote stretch of coastline that she first picked up a camera, mostly to document seabird colonies for her research notes. Gap 11 It wasn't long before the photographs themselves became the point.

What strikes people most about her work, she says, is not the animals but the patience behind each image. Gap 12 "You can't force a fox to walk into good light," she explains. "All you can do is be there, quietly, often for hours, and hope."

That approach has not always been easy to sustain. Early in her career, funding for long expeditions was scarce, and she often financed trips herself, sleeping in her car to save money. Gap 13 Looking back, she describes those years as formative rather than difficult, though she admits she wouldn't recommend the lifestyle to everyone.

Her most well-known image, of a snow leopard crossing a ridge in near-total darkness, took eleven days of waiting to capture. Gap 14 When the moment finally came, she says, she almost forgot to press the shutter.

These days, Elena spends part of her year mentoring younger photographers, several of whom she met through online forums rather than formal training. Gap 15 She is characteristically modest about her own influence, insisting that most of what she teaches is simply how to wait well.

Asked what keeps her going after two decades in remote and often uncomfortable locations, she doesn't hesitate. Gap 16 It is, she says, the closest thing she has found to a definition of her job.

Part 8Multiple Matching

You are going to read about four people who took up a musical instrument as adults. For questions 17–26, choose from the people A–D. The people may be chosen more than once.

A — Owen

I picked up the guitar at forty-five, mostly out of boredom, and assumed I'd give up within a month like most of my previous hobbies. Instead, I found practising strangely calming after long days at work, and I now play for twenty minutes most evenings, badly but happily. My one regret is not starting sooner — my fingers took far longer to toughen up than a teenager's would have.

B — Grace

I'd always wanted to play the violin as a child but never had the chance, so I finally signed up for lessons in my thirties. The hardest part wasn't the technique but managing my own embarrassment — I felt slightly ridiculous squeaking through beginner exercises at an age when I was used to being competent at things. My teacher has been endlessly encouraging, though, and I've stopped caring how I sound to the neighbours.

C — Felix

Learning the piano was entirely my daughter's idea — she starts lessons this year, and I decided I didn't want to just watch from the sofa. We now practise together most weekends, which has become one of my favourite parts of the week. What I hadn't expected was how much better it's made me at reading in general; apparently the two skills share more than people think.

D — Ingrid

After a stressful year at work, a friend suggested I try the drums as a way of letting off steam, and it turned out to be excellent advice. I'm still not particularly good, and I doubt I ever will be given how little time I have to practise, but that has stopped mattering to me. The forty-five minutes I spend at my kit each week are the only time I'm not thinking about anything else at all.
