Elena takes charge of the family bookshop

When her grandmother died, Elena inherited not only the bookshop on Cobbler's Lane but also a stock of unsold optimism that had accumulated for the better part of forty years. The shop smelled of dust and pipe tobacco long since extinguished, and the shelves sagged under editions nobody had asked for in a decade. Her cousins had assumed she would sell the premises within the month; property on that street had become absurdly valuable, and nobody, they reasoned, opened a bookshop in this day and age unless they were sentimental to the point of foolishness.

Elena was not, she told herself, being sentimental. She simply could not shake the sense that liquidating the place — handing it over to be gutted and turned into another shop selling scented candles — would be a kind of theft, though she could not have said from whom. Her grandmother had never once suggested that Elena take over; if anything, the old woman had been almost apologetic about the business, referring to it, on the rare occasions she mentioned it at all, as 'the millstone'. And yet she had kept it open, quarter after unprofitable quarter, long past the point where sentiment alone could explain it.

It took Elena the better part of a year to understand why. The bookshop, she came to see, was not a business that happened to sell books; it was an argument, conducted daily and without fanfare, that certain things ought to exist regardless of whether they paid for themselves. Her grandmother had never articulated this, because articulating it would have made it sound like a manifesto, and manifestos embarrassed her. She had simply gone on ordering the poetry nobody bought and the local history nobody read, and had trusted that the ledger would sort itself out, which, more often than not, it hadn't.

Elena's first instinct, on taking over, was to modernise: a coffee machine, a rearrangement of the window display, a website. She did all these things, and none of them made the slightest difference to the accounts, though the coffee machine did at least mean she no longer had to walk to the café next door. What changed the shop's fortunes, when they eventually changed, was something she had not planned at all. A customer, browsing the poetry section that her grandmother had stubbornly maintained despite years of losses, mentioned in passing that he ran a small reading group that met nowhere in particular because nowhere in particular would have them. Elena, more from politeness than strategy, offered the back room.

Within six months there were four such groups, meeting on different evenings, none of them paying rent, all of them buying books they would otherwise have borrowed from the library or not read at all. The accountant, when he saw the figures, was baffled; nothing about the arrangement made obvious commercial sense, and yet the shop was, for the first time in a decade, comfortably solvent. Elena found she could not fully explain this to him, any more than her grandmother could have explained the ledger, and she suspected that the explanation, if there was one, had less to do with commerce than with the fact that people will pay, eventually and indirectly, for a room that takes them seriously.

She thought of her grandmother often in those months, and of the word 'millstone', which she now understood rather differently. It had not been self-pity, she realised, nor even complaint, but something closer to a private joke — the old woman's way of acknowledging the absurdity of the enterprise without ever quite admitting how much it mattered to her. Elena, for her part, had stopped bothering to justify the shop to her cousins. It was, she had decided, none of their business why a room full of unfashionable books should be allowed to go on existing; it was enough that it did.

The River That Remembered Itself

For the first time in over four hundred years, the unmistakable slap of a beaver's tail against water can be heard along the Afon Einion, a modest river in the hills of central Wales. The animals were reintroduced sixteen months ago as part of a rewilding trial, and the changes since then have startled even the ecologists who proposed the project.

Translation: The Art of Faithful Betrayal

ATranslation is very nearly as old as writing itself. The earliest known translated texts include bilingual inscriptions from the ancient Near East, produced not for literary pleasure but for administrative necessity: treaties, trade agreements and royal decrees needed to be intelligible to more than one people if they were to have any force at all. Religious translation, too, has an ancient pedigree; sacred texts were rendered into new languages partly to spread doctrine and partly, more subtly, to anchor a foreign faith within a local culture, adapting its idiom until it felt, to new readers, as though it had always belonged to them. In this sense, translation has never been merely a technical exercise in substituting one vocabulary for another; from its earliest instances, it has been an act with the power to reshape the culture that receives it, sometimes as profoundly as it reflects the culture from which it came.

BFor centuries, translators and their critics have argued over where a translator's loyalty properly lies: with the exact words of the original, or with the effect those words were intended to produce. Scholars in classical antiquity already distinguished between rendering a text 'word for word' and rendering it 'sense for sense', and the tension between these two approaches has never been fully resolved. A translation that hews closely to the original's grammar and vocabulary risks producing something stilted or, worse, unintelligible in the new language; one that prioritises fluency and naturalness risks drifting so far from the original that it becomes, in effect, a new work bearing only a passing resemblance to its source. Every translator, whether they acknowledge it or not, makes a thousand small decisions that fall somewhere between these two poles.

CThere is a peculiar convention in publishing whereby the best translations are those readers do not notice at all: a good translator, it is often said, should be invisible, their intervention undetectable, so that readers feel they are encountering the author directly rather than through an intermediary. This standard, though widely held, sits uneasily with what translation actually requires, which is an act of literary creation in its own right. A translator must find, in an entirely different language, equivalents for wordplay, rhythm and cultural reference that frequently have no direct counterpart; where no equivalent exists, they must invent one, drawing on their own resources as a writer. It is telling that some of the most celebrated translations in literary history are prized precisely for the distinctive voice their translator brought to the task, even as the convention of invisibility persists in how such work is discussed and, all too often, credited.

DThe last decade has seen automated translation tools improve at a pace that would have seemed implausible only a generation ago, to the point where they now produce serviceable renderings of straightforward text almost instantaneously. Yet for all their fluency, these systems continue to falter precisely where translation is hardest: idiom, irony, ambiguity deliberately left unresolved, and register calibrated to a specific audience. A system trained overwhelmingly on the patterns of existing text has little basis for judging when a departure from those patterns is exactly what a passage requires, and can mistake a writer's studied peculiarity of style for an error to be smoothed away. Professional translators report that the tools are most useful as a rough first draft, one that still demands substantial human revision before it can be trusted with anything of consequence.

EIt seems unlikely, in the near term at least, that literary translation will be surrendered entirely to machines, though the boundary between human and automated work is shifting rapidly in less prestigious corners of the field, such as technical manuals and routine correspondence. What is emerging instead is a division of labour in which software handles volume and speed, while human translators are reserved for texts where nuance, cultural sensitivity or authorial voice genuinely matter. Whether this division proves stable is another question; as machine systems continue to improve, the category of texts considered to require a human touch may simply keep shrinking, paragraph by paragraph, until only the most demanding literary work remains reliably human territory — for now.
