The Luthier's Correction

Marco had been in Vidal's workshop for three years before he was permitted to touch a scroll unsupervised, and even then the old man watched from across the room with an attentiveness Marco found more unsettling than any direct correction. Vidal rarely raised his voice. He had, instead, a habit of falling silent at the precise moment an apprentice began to suspect he had gone wrong, a silence that communicated more disapproval than a shouted rebuke ever could have. Marco had learned to dread it far more than criticism, because criticism, at least, told you where you stood.

It was into one of these silences that Marco carved, on a Tuesday in late October, the scroll of a violin destined for a soloist in Lisbon. He had followed the template Vidal had given him with what he believed to be complete fidelity, and it was only when he set down his gouge and stepped back that he noticed the curve of the volute was, by some fraction of a millimetre he could not account for, asymmetrical. He considered, for a moment that felt considerably longer than it was, saying nothing. The flaw was minute; a soloist playing the instrument would never see it, and few clients, in Marco's experience, examined a scroll with anything like the scrutiny Vidal brought to the task. But he found he could not quite make himself stay silent, and when Vidal finally crossed the room, Marco spoke before the old man could reach for the piece himself.

Vidal turned the scroll over twice in his hands, saying nothing for long enough that Marco began to regret his honesty. Then, instead of the correction Marco had braced for, the old man asked him a question: not what had gone wrong, but why Marco had chosen to mention it at all, when he might easily have let it pass unnoticed. Marco, caught off guard, offered the only answer he had, which was that it would have felt like a kind of theft — taking payment for work he knew to be flawed, even in a way nobody else would ever detect. Vidal considered this for a moment and then set the scroll down without comment, which Marco had learned, by now, to interpret as something closer to approval than any word could have conveyed.

What Marco did not understand, not yet, was that the asymmetry itself had troubled Vidal far less than his apprentice supposed. Vidal had seen a hundred flawed scrolls in his career, his own included, and had long since made peace with the fact that the hand is never quite as obedient as the eye demands. What he had been testing, in his silence that afternoon, was not Marco's craftsmanship but something closer to his temperament: whether the boy possessed the particular, unglamorous discipline of a maker who corrects his own errors before anyone asks him to, rather than the more common instinct to hope they go unnoticed. It was a quality Vidal valued above raw talent, precisely because it could not be taught by demonstration; a master could show an apprentice how to hold a gouge, but not how to want, unprompted, to get a thing right.

The instrument, once finished, was unremarkable in every respect that mattered to a concert hall, and Marco knew this, and had made his peace with knowing it. What he did not fully grasp until years later, when he had apprentices of his own, was that Vidal's silence that Tuesday had not been a test he passed so much as a door, quietly and without ceremony, held open.

Sleeper Trains Rediscover Their Passengers

For much of the late twentieth century, the overnight sleeper train seemed destined for extinction, an inconvenient relic in an age of budget flights and high-speed rail. Across Europe, national operators quietly withdrew their sleeper services one by one, citing falling passenger numbers and the difficulty of justifying carriages that spent half their working life stationary in sidings.

A — A comparable revival has been observed in long-distance coach travel, where budget operators have reported steady growth in bookings among travellers unwilling to pay the premium associated with rail, though this growth has done little to dent demand for the newly relaunched sleeper routes.

Ticket sales quickly outstripped expectations, and images of the refurbished cabins, shared widely online, generated a level of public enthusiasm that surprised even ÖBB's own marketing department.
D — Part of the explanation lies in a broader unease about air travel's environmental cost. The Swedish term 'flygskam', or flight shame, entered common usage after climate activists began publicising the carbon footprint of short-haul flights, and surveys since have shown a growing minority of travellers willing to accept a longer journey in exchange for a clearer conscience.
E — In France, the once-extensive network of overnight services was reduced to a single surviving route by 2017; in Germany, Deutsche Bahn withdrew its City Night Line brand entirely the year before, handing its remaining carriages to an Austrian operator prepared to take on the financial risk that Deutsche Bahn itself was not.
F — Within two years, several Nightjet routes were selling out weeks in advance, particularly in the cheaper couchette compartments favoured by younger travellers, and waiting lists became common on routes that had previously struggled to fill a handful of berths.
G — Since 2021, new or restored sleeper connections have linked Paris and Berlin, Brussels and Prague, and Zurich and Rome, several of them the product of joint ventures between operators who had previously regarded one another chiefly as competitors.

Defaults: The Choices We Never Actually Make

ADefault options — the choice that is made automatically for us unless we actively opt out — have quietly become one of the most consequential tools in modern policy and technology alike. The now-famous contrast between countries with 'opt-in' organ donation, where citizens must actively register as donors, and those with 'opt-out' systems, where citizens are presumed to consent unless they explicitly withdraw, remains the textbook illustration: donation rates in opt-out countries are often dramatically higher, despite no difference in the underlying willingness of citizens to donate when asked directly. The idea entered mainstream policy discussion largely through the work of behavioural economists Richard Thaler and Cass Sunstein, whose 2008 book popularised the notion of the 'nudge' — a gentle architectural adjustment to the choices people face, one that steers behaviour without ever forbidding an alternative.

BWhat makes defaults so powerful is not, as is sometimes assumed, simple laziness on the part of the chooser. Changing a default requires a person to notice that a choice exists at all, to judge that the alternative is worth the effort of switching, and then to act on that judgement — three separate hurdles, any one of which is enough to keep the default in place. This is compounded by a further psychological tendency, well documented long before defaults became fashionable in policy circles: people tend to interpret the default option as an implicit recommendation, reasoning that whoever designed the system probably had good reason to set things up that way. The effect persists even among people who, if asked directly, insist that the default carries no such endorsement.

CIt is this apparent endorsement effect that has made defaults ethically contentious. Critics argue that exploiting a person's inertia to achieve an outcome — even a beneficial one, such as higher organ donation rates or greater retirement savings — sits uneasily with the ideal of autonomous choice; if people are not genuinely weighing the alternatives, can their resulting decision really be called their own? Defenders counter that neutrality is an illusion in any case: some default must be set, since a system cannot present a choice without a starting position, and if a default is unavoidable, choosing the one that produces better outcomes for the average person seems preferable to leaving the matter to chance or to whichever option was easiest to programme.

DThe debate has taken on sharper edges as defaults have migrated from public policy into commercial software design, where the incentives of the party setting the default are considerably less aligned with the interests of the person choosing. Subscription services routinely default to automatic renewal, buried several menus deep from the cancellation option; social media platforms default to the most permissive privacy settings, on the reasonable assumption that most users will never go looking for the stricter alternative. Designers within the industry have coined the term 'dark pattern' for defaults and interface choices deliberately engineered to produce outcomes that benefit the company at the user's expense, a practice that regulators have been notably slower to address than its ubiquity would seem to warrant.

ESeveral jurisdictions have begun legislating against the more egregious of these practices, typically by requiring that cancellation be no more difficult than the original sign-up, or that certain defaults — particularly around data collection — favour the more privacy-protective option unless the user actively chooses otherwise. Such rules implicitly accept the core insight of default research: that where the starting point is set will determine, for a great many people, where they end up, regardless of what they might have chosen under different circumstances. What remains unresolved is a harder question, one that policy has so far avoided answering directly: whether a tool this effective at shaping behaviour should ever be left to organisations whose interests point away from the public's own.
