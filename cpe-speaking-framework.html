Cambridge C2 Proficiency — Speaking

Don't wing it.
Run the frame.

You already have the vocabulary and the grammar — that was never the problem. What's missing under pressure is a shape to fall back on: a fixed sequence of moves for each part, so that when your mind goes blank you have somewhere to put your feet. This page gives you one repeatable structure per part, the exact connecting language to glue it together, and the criteria the examiner is actually ticking off while you talk.

Part 1 · 2 minutes

The interview

Two or three background questions each, examiner-led. The trap at C2 is answering like a B2 candidate: correct, but flat and short. The examiner isn't testing information — they're testing whether you can develop an answer unprompted.

EDGE
Engage → Develop → Give an example → Extend. Four moves, roughly 4 sentences, every time you're asked anything.
E

Engage — answer immediately, no padding

Direct answer in your first sentence. Don't circle the question before landing on it.

"I'd say I'm reasonably good at it, actually — though it depends on the week."
D

Develop — add the reason

One sentence of justification. This is where a linking word does the C2 signalling for you.

"Mainly because my work forces a routine on me whether I like it or not."
G

Give a concrete example

A specific instance beats a general claim. This is the sentence that stops the answer sounding rehearsed.

"Last month, for instance, I had to fit three deadlines into one week and somehow it worked."
X

Extend — widen it or qualify it

A short pivot outward: a contrast, a concession, or a link to the next likely question. This is the sentence that sounds like a real opinion rather than an exam answer.

"Having said that, I don't think I'd cope half as well without deadlines forcing my hand."
Under time pressure

If a question catches him off guard, the fix isn't a better answer — it's a stalling phrase that still counts as fluent speech. See the Buying time section of the Language bank. Silence costs marks; a well-placed "That's an interesting one, actually…" does not.

Part 2 · 1 min + 3 min

The collaborative task

Two candidates, one picture booklet, no examiner input while you talk. This part is graded most heavily on Interactive Communication — initiating, responding, and negotiating with your partner — not on who describes the pictures best.

≈ 1 min
Two selected pictures — how common is this in your country?
≈ 3 min
All pictures — discuss the issues, agree which is most interesting
SCAN
Survey → Compare → Assess → Nominate. Same shape for both halves of the task — the second half just runs it across more pictures and ends in a joint decision.
S

Survey — name what's happening, fast

One sentence per picture, no more. This is scene-setting, not the task itself — don't let him linger here.

"So in this one we've got someone running a drinks stall, and here it looks like beekeepers at work."
C

Compare — pull the pictures against each other

The actual task word is "compare" or "how common" — so a comparative structure should appear early, and often.

"I'd say the market stall is far more typical where I'm from than beekeeping, which feels quite niche in comparison."
A

Assess — bring in the underlying issue

For the second half specifically: don't just describe the picture, name the issue behind it (working conditions, job security, seasonal work, hygiene, exploitation…).

"What strikes me about this one is the question of how regulated that kind of work actually is."
N

Nominate — reach the decision together, out loud

The task always ends in a joint choice. Say the negotiation, don't just arrive at an answer — that's what's being marked.

"Between the two of us I think we're leaning towards the food safety angle — would you agree, or do you feel more strongly about the other one?"
The one habit worth drilling

Every SCAN cycle should end with a question or an invitation back to the partner — "What do you make of this one?", "Do you see it the same way?". That single habit is what separates a C2 Interactive Communication score from a C1 one: he's not answering the task, he's running a conversation about it.

Part 3 · long turn (2 min) → comment (1 min) → discussion (4 min)

The long turn, and what follows it

He gets a card with a question and three bullet prompts. This is the part that rewards a teacher's instinct — the card is already an essay outline. Treat it exactly like one, spoken.

FRAME
Frame the question → Run each bullet → Answer directly. The three bullets on the card are not decoration — they are the three body paragraphs.
1

Frame the question — 10 seconds, no more

Restate the question in his own words before answering it. This buys thinking time legitimately and shows control of the topic from sentence one.

"So the question is really whether it's better to trust your own judgement or lean on other people's — and I think it depends heavily on the context."
2

Run each bullet as its own mini-paragraph

Signal the move to a new bullet explicitly — this is what makes the two minutes sound organised rather than a stream of thought. Three bullets, three clearly marked sections, roughly 30–35 seconds each.

"If we start with education… / Turning to the workplace, though… / And then there's the question of age…"
3

Answer the question directly at the end

Don't let the long turn trail off after the last bullet. One closing sentence that actually answers the original question — examiners consistently mark this down when it's missing.

"So on balance, I'd say the older you get, the more your own judgement should be trusted — but that trust has to be earned early on by listening to others."

Then — commenting on your partner (1 min)

  • Agree and extend rather than just agreeing: add a new angle, don't repeat theirs.
  • If you disagree, disagree on one specific point, not the whole answer.
  • Always end back on the examiner's follow-up question — don't drift.

Then — general discussion (up to 4 min)

  • This is the most abstract stretch of the whole test — questions move from personal to societal.
  • Push answers up a level of abstraction: not "I think X" but "It seems to me that, generally speaking, X" — hedged, general claims score higher here than anecdotes.
  • This is pure Interactive Communication territory again — bring the partner in, don't monologue.
Reusable across all three parts

The language bank

These are the joints that connect the moves above. Practise them until they're automatic — at C2 the examiner is listening for range and naturalness in exactly this kind of connective tissue.

Buying time (without going silent)

"That's a good question, actually…"
"Let me think about that for a second…"
"I haven't really considered that before, but…"
"There are a couple of ways to look at this…"

Hedging an opinion (sounds native, protects you when unsure)

"I'd tend to say…"
"On the whole, I'd argue…"
"It strikes me that…"
"I could well be wrong, but…"
"There's probably an argument that…"

Bringing your partner in (Part 2 & Part 3 discussion)

"What's your take on this?"
"Would you go along with that?"
"Do you see it the same way, or…?"
"I take your point, but I'd push back slightly on…"
"Building on what you said…"

Signposting a structured answer (Part 3 long turn)

"If we start with…"
"Moving on to…"
"That said…"
"Which brings me to…"
"On balance, then…"

Self-correcting cleanly (fluent recovery, not restart)

"Or rather…"
"What I mean is…"
"Sorry — let me rephrase that…"
What the examiner is actually scoring

The five marks behind every answer

Every one of the moves above exists to serve one of these. Worth reading through once so the frameworks stop feeling arbitrary.

Grammatical Resource
Range and accuracy of structures. EDGE's "Extend" step and hedging language are what surface a wider range of structures than a plain answer would.
Lexical Resource
Already his strength — the frameworks exist to give that vocabulary somewhere organised to land, so it reads as control rather than a list.
Discourse Management
Coherence, length, and organisation of turns. This is exactly what FRAME and the signposting bank are built for — a two-minute turn that sounds planned, not rambled.
Pronunciation
Stress, intonation, individual sounds — clear enough that no effort is needed to understand him. Not addressed by structure; needs separate, targeted practice.
Interactive Communication
Initiating, responding, turn-taking, negotiating meaning. This is the whole point of SCAN's "Nominate" step and the "bringing your partner in" language — Part 2 and the Part 3 discussion are scored almost entirely on this.
