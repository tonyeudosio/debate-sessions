Quick recap

How the paper is built

Part 1 is not optional — everyone writes the same kind of text. Part 2 gives you a choice, but only from a set of task types that rotate exam to exam.

Part 1

Essay — compulsory

Everyone answers this. You're given a statement, two notes on what to write about, and a blank third note for your own idea.

  • 140–190 words
  • Must give a clear opinion, with reasons
  • Must use both given notes + your own idea
  • Worth 20 of the 40 marks

How you'll actually grade it

The marking scale, in plain language

Every piece of writing gets four separate marks, 0–5 each, not one overall impression. A student can nail the content and still lose marks on language — the scale is built to show you exactly where.

CriterionWhat it's really asking
ContentDid they answer everything the task asked for — no missing points, nothing off-topic? Does the reader end up with all the information they needed?
Communicative AchievementDoes it actually read like the text type it's supposed to be? An email that sounds like an essay, or an article that opens "Dear Sir," loses marks here even if the grammar is perfect.
OrganisationIs it easy to follow? Are ideas linked together, not just listed? Paragraphing and linking words live in this box.
LanguageRange and accuracy of vocabulary and grammar. Small mistakes that don't block meaning are fine — the question is always "can the reader still understand?"

5 Strong

  • Every point covered, nothing spare
  • Reads naturally in its genre
  • Varied linking, rich vocabulary
  • Slips don't confuse the reader

3 Solid B2

  • Minor gaps or extra detail
  • Genre conventions mostly right
  • Coherent, some cohesive devices
  • Everyday vocabulary, controlled grammar

1 Still B1

  • Some misreading of the task
  • Genre conventions patchy
  • Basic linking only
  • Errors are noticeable but meaning survives

0 Off-task

  • Doesn't address the question
  • Reader is left uninformed
  • Below Band 1 in every other respect

Bands 2 and 4 sit between these — a mix of Band 1 and Band 3 features, or Band 3 and Band 5. A candidate averaging Band 3 or above is performing at genuine B2 level; Bands 1–2 still read as B1. You'll find a ready-to-fill version of this scale attached to every task in the practice zone below.

Part 1 · compulsory · 140–190 words

The Essay

An opinion essay built from a statement and two given notes, plus a third idea of the student's own. Everyone answers this one, so it's worth over-preparing.

In your English class you've been discussing social media. Your teacher has asked you to write an essay for homework.

Write your essay using all the notes and giving reasons for your point of view.

"Social media does teenagers more harm than good." Do you agree?

Notes — write about: 1. communication with friends  2. mental health  3. ______ (your own idea)
  1. Introduction — paraphrase the statement, state your opinion clearly from the start
  2. Given note 1 — one idea, developed with a reason or example
  3. Given note 2 — same treatment, don't just list it
  4. Your own idea — a full paragraph, not a throwaway line
  5. Conclusion — restate your opinion in different words, close firmly

Swap it: essay register

I think...In my opinion... / I firmly believe...
But...However, ... / Nevertheless, ...
Also...Furthermore, ... / What is more, ...
A lot of peopleMany people / The majority of people
In the end...To conclude, ... / Taking everything into account, ...
Annotated sample — Band 5

Nowadays, most teenagers spend a considerable part of their day on social media, and in my opinion, its drawbacks are beginning to outweigh its benefits.1

Firstly, while platforms like Instagram and WhatsApp were designed to bring people closer, they can just as easily replace real conversation2 — many teenagers now prefer texting a friend sitting right next to them rather than simply talking. Furthermore3, constant exposure to carefully edited images of other people's lives has been linked to lower self-esteem and rising anxiety among young people4, which is a genuinely worrying trend.

What is more, in my view6, the endless scrolling itself eats into time that could be spent sleeping, studying or exercising — often without teenagers even noticing how much time has passed.5

Of course, social media also has undeniable advantages, such as staying in touch with family abroad. Nevertheless7, I firmly believe that, for most teenagers, the negative effects on wellbeing are far harder to ignore.8

1Organisation — paraphrases the statement and states an opinion immediately, so the whole essay has a clear direction from line one.
2Content — given note 1 (communication) is addressed head-on, not just mentioned in passing.
3Language — "Furthermore" instead of another "Also" — range in linking devices.
4Content — given note 2 (mental health) covered with a concrete reason, not just named.
5Content — this whole paragraph is the candidate's own idea, the required third note — missing this costs marks even if the other two are excellent.
6Language — "in my view" instead of a third "I think" — varying the opinion language on purpose.
7Organisation — "Of course... Nevertheless" acknowledges the other side before returning to the argument — examiners reward this at the top band.
8Communicative Achievement — the conclusion restates the opinion in new words and closes firmly, matching essay register throughout.

Language bank — opinion & linking

Opinion: In my view / As far as I'm concerned / I am convinced that Addition: Furthermore / Moreover / What is more Contrast: Nevertheless / On the other hand / Having said that Conclusion: To conclude / All things considered / Taking everything into account Emphasis: Undoubtedly / It goes without saying that

Watch out for

  • Copying the essay title as the opening sentence instead of paraphrasing it.
  • Skipping a given note because the student disagrees with it — they must discuss it, not necessarily agree.
  • Squeezing the "own idea" into one clause instead of giving it a full paragraph.
  • Contractions and casual words (a lot of, stuff, kids) — essay register is semi-formal.
  • Sitting on the fence — the task always asks for a clear opinion.
Part 2 · optional · 140–190 words

Email & Letter

One task type, two possible registers. The exam gives you an email or letter to react to, plus notes — your job is choosing the right tone as much as answering the points.

You have just received an email from your English-speaking friend, Jordan.

"Hi! I heard you went to a summer language school abroad — how was it? I've been thinking about doing something similar next year. Would you recommend the school? Let me know!"

Write your email, using all the notes.

Notes — write about: what you learned  ·  the school itself  ·  ______ (your own idea)
  1. Greeting — Hi/Dear + name (informal) or Dear Mr/Ms Surname (formal)
  2. Opening line — react briefly to what they told you
  3. Respond to each point — in the order they were raised
  4. Weave in your own idea — naturally, not bolted on
  5. Closing + sign-off — must match the greeting's register

Swap it: informal → formal

Hi Jordan,Dear Mr Reyes, / Dear Sir or Madam,
Thanks a lot!I would be very grateful if...
I can't wait to...I very much look forward to...
Let me know!I look forward to hearing from you.
See you soon, / Love,Yours sincerely, / Yours faithfully,

Rule of thumb: know the person's name → Yours sincerely. Wrote "Dear Sir/Madam" → Yours faithfully.

Annotated sample — informal register, Band 5

Hi Jordan,1

Great to hear from you — and yes, definitely, I'd recommend it!2 I learned way more than I expected, mostly because the classes were tiny and the teachers pushed us to actually speak from day one, not just do grammar exercises.3

As for the school, it was based right in the town centre, so we could practise in real shops and cafés after lessons, which honestly helped more than the classroom itself.4 One thing worth knowing — book early, because the good host families get taken fast, so I'd sort accommodation the moment you decide to go.5

Anyway, I can't recommend it enough — let me know if you want the school's website and I'll dig it out for you.6

Speak soon,
Alex7

1Communicative Achievement — first-name greeting matches an email between close friends.
2Content — reacts to Jordan's question before anything else — the natural first move in a reply.
3Content — answers point 1 (what you learned) with a specific, personal detail, not a vague sentence.
4Content — point 2 (the school) answered clearly, with real detail.
5Content — the candidate's own added idea (booking early) — shows initiative beyond the two given notes.
6Language — contractions and casual phrasing ("can't," "dig it out") kept consistent throughout — no slips into formal register.
7Organisation — informal sign-off matches the informal greeting used at the top.

Language bank

Informal open: It was great to hear from you / Thanks for your email Informal close: Write back soon! / Take care Formal open: I am writing in response to... / I am writing to inform you that... Formal close: I look forward to hearing from you Polite request: I would be grateful if you could...

Watch out for

  • Mixing registers mid-text — one contraction in an otherwise formal letter stands out badly.
  • Sign-off that doesn't match the greeting (Dear Sir/Madam... Love, Alex ❌).
  • Opening a formal letter like an essay ("Nowadays, many people...") instead of stating the purpose directly.
  • Missing one of the points raised in the original email — every point needs a response.
Part 2 · optional · 140–190 words

Article

Written for a magazine or website — the one genre where the exam actually wants personality. Grab attention, stay engaging, round it off.

You see this notice on an English-language website for teenagers:

"Travel — what's the most memorable trip you've ever been on? Write us an article about it, and tell us what made it so special. The best articles will be published on the site."

Write your article.

  1. Title — short, specific, makes someone want to click
  2. Hook — a question, direct address, or surprising line
  3. Body 1 — the main idea, with a real example
  4. Body 2 — a second angle or a practical tip
  5. Round off — echo the opening, invite the reader in

Swap it: flat → engaging

The trip was interesting.Ever taken a trip that completely changed how you see a place?
I like travelling.There's nothing quite like the moment a new city stops feeling foreign.
This is good advice.If you're planning your first trip alone, this one's for you.
In conclusion,So, next time a strange destination tempts you, say yes.
Annotated sample — Band 5

The Trip That Got Lost, On Purpose

Ever taken a trip where absolutely nothing went to plan — and it turned out to be the best part?1 That's exactly what happened when my family and I missed our connecting train in a tiny village in Portugal.2

With no train until the next morning, we ended up wandering the village instead, and a local baker insisted on giving us a tour after we got hopelessly lost looking for somewhere to eat.3 None of it was planned, and that's precisely why it's the trip I still talk about years later.4

If you ever get the chance, don't panic when travel plans fall apart.5 Some of the best memories happen exactly when the itinerary doesn't.

So, next time your plans collapse halfway through a trip, try leaning into it instead of fighting it.6 You might just end up with a story worth telling.7

1Communicative Achievement — a rhetorical question hooks the reader in the very first line, a classic article opener.
2Content — answers the notice's actual question directly, with a specific, real example.
3Content — concrete, memorable detail (the baker, the tour) instead of a vague summary of the trip.
4Organisation — ties the anecdote back to why it matters, rather than just narrating and moving on.
5Communicative Achievement — direct address to the reader ("you") plus a practical takeaway — engages rather than just informs.
6Organisation — the conclusion echoes the opening idea (plans falling apart) instead of just summarising — a strong closing technique.
7Language — a light, informal closing line ("a story worth telling") keeps the friendly magazine voice right to the end.

Language bank

Hooks: Have you ever...? / Picture this: / Believe it or not,... Vivid adjectives: breathtaking / unforgettable / downright chaotic Direct address: If you're anything like me,... / You might be thinking... Rounding off: So, why not give it a try? / Whatever you choose,...

Watch out for

  • No title, or a bland one ("My Holiday Essay").
  • Opening with "In this article I will talk about..." — too academic for the genre.
  • Never actually addressing the reader directly ("you").
  • Drifting away from the notice's real question.
  • Ending abruptly instead of rounding the idea off.
Part 2 · optional (standard version) · 140–190 words

Report

Written for someone with authority to act on it — a principal, a manager, a committee. Objective, headed, and built to be scanned, not enjoyed.

You have been asked by your college principal to write a report about the school library.

You should say how it is currently used, describe any problems, and suggest improvements.

Write your report.

  1. Heading — "Report on..." states the subject plainly
  2. Introduction — purpose and method in a sentence or two
  3. Findings, subheaded — group by theme, not chronology
  4. Second findings section — problems or gaps identified
  5. Recommendations — clear, actionable, easy to scan

Swap it: opinion → neutral report register

I think the library is boring.The library appears to be underused by students.
Everyone says...The majority of students surveyed reported...
You should buy more books.It is recommended that the book collection be expanded.
Basically,...Overall,...
Annotated sample — Band 5

Report on the School Library

Introduction
The purpose of this report is to assess how the school library is currently used and to suggest possible improvements, based on a short survey completed by 35 pupils.1

Current Use
At present, the library is mainly used during free periods, and mostly for group work rather than quiet reading.2 According to the survey, most students would like to use it more, but several noted that the English-language book selection is quite limited.3

Areas for Improvement
The main issue raised was noise: without a designated quiet zone, students who want to study in silence often give up and leave.4 A number of students also mentioned that opening hours end too early for anyone with after-school clubs.

Recommendations
Based on these findings, it is recommended that the library create a separate quiet study area, extend its opening hours by an hour, and add more English-language fiction.5

1Organisation — headings plus a clear statement of purpose and method set report register from the first line.
2Content — factual, itemised information delivered plainly, with no personal opinion language.
3Content — the gap identified is backed by survey evidence, not a personal complaint.
4Communicative Achievement — impersonal phrasing ("was raised," "was noted") is exactly the register a report needs — an article or email would sound wrong here.
5Organisation — a clearly headed Recommendations section with distinct, actionable points — easy for a busy reader to scan.

Language bank

Purpose: The purpose of this report is to... / This report aims to... Reporting findings: It was found that... / Respondents indicated that... Recommending: It is recommended that... / The school could consider... Headings: Introduction / Current Use / Areas for Improvement / Recommendations

Watch out for

  • Writing in first-person opinion throughout ("I think... I feel...").
  • No headings — a report should never look like a wall of text.
  • Asserting things without framing them as findings ("The library is bad" vs "The library was reported to be...").
  • Missing a clearly separate Recommendations section.
  • A chatty, informal tone that belongs in an email, not a report.
Part 2 · optional · 140–190 words

Review

Part description, part verdict. The reader wants to know what something was like and whether they should bother — both halves need to be there.

You see this announcement on an English-language website:

"Reviews wanted! Have you listened to a podcast recently that you'd recommend — or wouldn't? Write us a review saying what it was like, and whether other readers should give it a try."

Write your review.

  1. Title — tells the reader what's being reviewed
  2. Intro — what it is, brief context
  3. Description — what you actually experienced
  4. Evaluation — strengths and weaknesses, balanced
  5. Verdict — clear recommendation, who it suits

Swap it: bland → evaluative

The episodes are good.The episodes are genuinely gripping from start to finish.
It is long.Some episodes drag on a little longer than they need to.
I recommend it.I'd wholeheartedly recommend it to anyone who...
It was bad.It fell rather flat, unfortunately.
Annotated sample — Band 5

Mystery Hour: Worth Your Commute?

If you're after a podcast that turns a boring bus ride into the best part of your day, Mystery Hour might just be it — I've been hooked since episode one.1

Each episode unpacks a real unsolved case, and the host has a knack for building suspense without ever feeling cheap or exaggerated.2 The sound design deserves a special mention too — creaking doors and distant footsteps make it genuinely atmospheric, even through cheap earphones.3

That said, a few episodes run close to an hour, which can feel like a lot if you're only half paying attention.4

Still, if you enjoy a good mystery and don't mind the occasional long episode, I'd say give it a go — just maybe start with a shorter one first.5

1Communicative Achievement — title plus a hook line establishes context before the evaluation even starts.
2Language — precise, evaluative vocabulary ("a knack for," "without feeling cheap") is a hallmark of strong reviews.
3Content — specific features named and evaluated individually, not a vague "it was good."
4Organisation — "That said" signals balance — strengths and weaknesses both covered, which a fair review needs.
5Communicative Achievement — a clear final verdict aimed at a specific kind of reader — the genre's defining move.

Language bank

Positive: gripping / thoroughly enjoyable / a hidden gem Negative: a letdown / drags on / underwhelming Comparing: nowhere near as good as / far superior to Recommending: well worth a listen / I'd give it a miss

Watch out for

  • Pure description with no personal opinion at all.
  • Only positives, or only negatives — a fair review balances both.
  • No clear recommendation at the end.
  • Missing a title.
  • Sounding like a report — lists of facts instead of an evaluative voice.
Part 2 · optional (Schools version) · 140–190 words

Story

A given opening line plus two required elements to weave in naturally. Tense control does more work here than in any other task type.

You have seen this announcement in an English-language magazine for teenagers.

Your story must begin with this sentence: "The lights flickered twice, then died completely, plunging the whole room into darkness."

Your story must include:

a mistake  ·  a locked door
  1. Opening — use the given line to set the scene immediately
  2. Development — build tension, work the required elements in naturally
  3. Turning point — a complication or decision
  4. Climax — the peak moment
  5. Resolution — a real ending, not a fade-out

Swap it: flat narration → vivid narrative

She was scared.Her heart hammered against her ribs.
Then something happened.Without warning, the door creaked open.
She waited two hours before he came.She had been waiting for nearly two hours when he finally arrived.
The end.[close on a final image or realisation instead]
Annotated sample — Band 5

The lights flickered twice, then died completely, plunging the whole room into darkness.1

Lucia froze. She had been the last one in the archive room, and now she couldn't even see the door she'd walked through five minutes earlier.2 Feeling her way along the shelves, she finally reached it — only to find it wouldn't budge. It had locked automatically, exactly as the librarian had warned her it might.3

Then she remembered: she'd been so eager to finish her essay early that she'd ignored the sign asking students to prop the door open after hours.4 That one careless decision now had her trapped in a windowless room with a dead phone battery.

Refusing to panic, she felt along the wall until her fingers found a fire alarm switch. She hesitated for only a second before pulling it.5

Within minutes, footsteps thundered down the corridor, and the door was finally pulled open. "Never," said the librarian, catching her breath, "ignore that sign again."6

1Content — opens with the exact given first line, as the task requires.
2Language — past perfect ("had been," "she'd walked") correctly sequences events relative to the main timeline.
3Content — the required element (a locked door) enters naturally within the action, not bolted on.
4Content — the required element (a mistake) is explicit and central to the plot, not incidental.
5Organisation — a clear turning point (the decision to pull the alarm) drives the story toward its climax.
6Communicative Achievement — ending on a line of dialogue gives the story a real, satisfying close rather than fading out.

Language bank

Tenses: past simple (main events) / past continuous (background) / past perfect (earlier events) Descriptive verbs: froze / thundered / fumbled / hesitated Building tension: Without warning,... / Suddenly,... / Little did she know... Pacing: short sentences for tension, longer ones for description

Watch out for

  • Forgetting one of the required elements from the prompt.
  • Past simple only, throughout — flat, with no real sense of sequence.
  • Not actually starting with the given first line.
  • A rushed or unclear ending.
  • Dialogue without correct punctuation (new line per speaker, quotation marks).

Essay — screen time limits

140–190 words

In your English class you've been talking about technology. Your teacher has asked you to write an essay: "Parents should limit how much time teenagers spend on their phones." Do you agree? Notes: 1. sleep and concentration  2. trust and independence  3. ______ (your own idea)

Aim for 140–190 words0 words
Show model answer & marking notes

Phone use among teenagers is a genuinely tricky subject, and while I understand parents' concerns, I don't believe strict limits are always the right answer.

To begin with, it's true that late-night scrolling affects sleep and makes it harder to concentrate the next day — this is well documented and hard to dismiss. Equally, though, constantly monitoring a teenager's phone time can damage the trust between parent and child, making them feel treated like a young kid rather than someone learning to manage their own time.

What is more, I believe teenagers learn self-control far better through open conversation about why limits matter than through rules imposed without explanation.

To conclude, while some guidance is clearly useful, I think teaching responsibility works better than simply restricting screen time.

Quick check: clear opinion from line one · both given notes developed, not listed · own idea gets its own paragraph · varied linking (To begin with / Equally / What is more / To conclude).

Article — a skill worth learning

140–190 words

You see this notice on a website: "Skills — what's one skill you think every teenager should learn, and why? Write us an article!" Write your article.

Aim for 140–190 words0 words
Show model answer & marking notes

The One Skill Nobody Teaches You in School

Can you cook a proper meal without following a recipe step by step? If not, you're not alone — and that's exactly the skill I think every teenager should learn.

Cooking isn't just about food. It teaches patience, planning, and — perhaps most importantly — how to manage a budget, since eating out constantly adds up fast. I only really learned this once I started making my own lunches, and it completely changed how I think about money.

It doesn't have to be complicated either. Start with three or four simple dishes you can make from memory, and build from there.

So next time you're tempted to order takeaway again, maybe give the kitchen a chance instead. Future you will be grateful.

Quick check: title makes a promise · opens with a hook, not a fact statement · personal, specific example · direct address to reader in the closing line.

Story — the wrong bag

140–190 words

Your story must begin with this sentence: "It wasn't until the bus had already left the stop that Maya realised the bag on her lap wasn't hers." Your story must include: a surprise and a phone call.

Aim for 140–190 words0 words
Show model answer & marking notes

It wasn't until the bus had already left the stop that Maya realised the bag on her lap wasn't hers. Same colour, same brand — but the initials stitched on the front definitely weren't hers.

Panicking slightly, she unzipped it, hoping to find a name or a phone. Instead, she found an envelope stuffed with old photographs and, folded on top, a plane ticket to somewhere she'd never even heard of. She hadn't expected that at all.

Just then, the phone inside the bag began to ring. Hesitating only a moment, Maya answered it.

"Hello? Oh — you're not my daughter," said a shaky voice on the other end. "I think we've swapped bags. Don't worry, dear — just tell me where you are, and I'll come and find you."

Quick check: opens with the exact given line · both required elements (surprise, phone call) are central, not decorative · mixed narrative tenses used correctly · ending lands on dialogue rather than fading out.
